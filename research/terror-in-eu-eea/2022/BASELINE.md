# Calendar-year 2022 baseline — 18 November 2026

Europol's full **TE-SAT 2023** covers the reference period 1 January–31 December 2022 (p. 88). The revised October edition identifies itself as superseding the prior edition (p. 2). The EU Publications Office lists DOI **10.2813/302117**, ISBN 978-92-95220-91-1 and a repository release date of 13 December 2023. The [source register](sources.csv) records both 94-page full PDFs inspected on 18 November 2026, their sizes and hashes, and the 404 from the legacy Europol download URL cited by the Commission. The revised bytes came from the Polish Police Terrorism Prevention Centre of Excellence. The superseded June PDF came from a separate mirror and has DOI 10.2813/370206. The hashes identify the retrieved bytes. The attack and arrest annexes (pp. 79–80) match in extracted text across both copies.

Seven countries have attack rows in [the annex transcription](attacks.csv): Italy 12, France 6, Greece 4, Belgium 3, and Germany, Slovakia and Spain one each. [Arrests](arrests.csv) sum to 380 across 16 states: 266 jihadist, 45 right-wing, 19 left-wing/anarchist, 18 separatist, 26 other and six unspecified. Arrests concern individuals suspected of terrorism-related offences, with no one-to-one link to attacks. The separatist zero is the published edition aggregate, subject to the [retrospective reporting audit](CROSS_YEAR_REPORTING_AUDIT.md). Missing national rows remain blank.

[Victim observations](statistics.csv) preserve four fatalities, including one each in Belgium and France and two in Slovakia. The right-wing overview on pp. 9 and 43 uses the ambiguous wording “two casualties”; the specific Bratislava account on p. 44 records two deaths and one injury, corroborated by the Slovak Special Prosecutor. Europol separately records one injury in the Spanish left-wing series (pp. 9 and 60). At least two injuries can therefore be reconstructed from explicit Europol case figures, with no verified EU-wide injury total. The reported injury in Schaarbeek is a separate national case observation.

National legislation governs Member State inputs; the report's ideological labels serve analytical purposes and have no formal legal effect (pp. 88–90 and n. 60). National practice in separating extremism from terrorism varies, especially in the left-wing series (pp. 9, 56). Qualitative examples can also include extremist incidents and events outside the EU or reference year. The [crosswalk](crosswalk.csv) accounts for 12 adequate matches, three candidates and 13 unidentified slots; case descriptions and scope are in [incidents.csv](incidents.csv). Spain's six linked physical acts take one statistical slot, as indicated by its sole completed annex entry and country-specific description on pp. 59–60. Arles and Schaarbeek remain candidates because the aggregate country and casualty patterns have no explicit incident-level match in the report. The Carasco narrative describes the event in context without expressly assigning its statistical status.

The [country register](coverage.csv) covers 31 countries. Northern Ireland's PSNI figures are calendar-year and separately defined, including one security-related death, five bombing incidents, 29 shootings and 110 security-linked Section 41 arrests. Great Britain coverage is case-based here (Dover), as are Norway (Oslo) and Iceland (a prosecution ending in acquittal on the terrorism counts). Liechtenstein's national statutory count of three terrorism cases is reported by the Liechtenstein-Institut from police statistics; attack outcomes were not established. These measures remain separate from the EU statistics.
