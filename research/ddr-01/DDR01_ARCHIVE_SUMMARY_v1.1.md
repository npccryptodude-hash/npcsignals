# DDR-01 Archive Summary v1.1

**Demographic Change Does Not Move Elections in One Direction**

## Status

**v1.1 — UPDATED 21 SEPTEMBER 2026**

v1.0 remains preserved with its original freeze point of 19 September 2026.

This update incorporates Germany's September 2026 state-election sequence.

## Question

Does demographic change alter electoral competition in a structurally observable way?

## Result

Yes — through identifiable mechanisms.

But the evidence does not support a universal political direction or a single cross-country effect size.

## Germany 2026 update

The September German state-election sequence is now treated as completed for DDR-01 observational purposes.

Saxony-Anhalt voted on 6 September.

Berlin and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern voted on 20 September.

The Federal Returning Officer lists the next scheduled Land elections in April 2027.

The three September observations did not produce one common political direction.

Reuters reported:

- AfD finished first in Saxony-Anhalt with nearly 44% and 39 of 83 seats.
- AfD finished first in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, reported at 38.3%.
- The Left Party finished first in Berlin with 25.3%; AfD received 16.3%.

The German sequence therefore does not support a simple claim that one demographic or immigration-related mechanism alone determines electoral direction.

Economic conditions, regional history, dissatisfaction with government, foreign policy, housing and other local issues were also relevant in the reporting surrounding these elections.

Germany strengthens the case for separating demographic structure from political reaction and from final electoral outcome.

It does not establish a demographic causal effect size.

## Sweden 2026

Sweden remains the completed national-election validation case.

The Swedish Election Authority formally established the Riksdag result on 19 September 2026. A total of 6,834,413 people voted and turnout was 84.9%.

DDR-01 does not treat the Swedish result as proof of demographic causation. It is one observation inside the comparative framework.

## What survived the stress test

**Direct channel:** ROBUST  
Demographic change can alter the participating electorate through population share, eligibility, naturalisation, registration, turnout, preference, geography and institutional conversion.

**Indirect channel:** SUPPORTED / EVIDENCE-CONCENTRATED  
Demographic change can alter issue salience, party positioning and the behaviour of voters outside the changing demographic group.

**Fixed directional model:** FALSIFIED  
The evidence does not support a universal rule that demographic change mechanically benefits one political side.

**Composition-only model:** INSUFFICIENT  
Population share without participation, preference, geography, issue salience and institutional context is not enough.

**Universal causal magnitude:** UNRESOLVED  
The test identifies mechanisms. It does not establish one common effect size across countries.

## Signal classification

**NOT A SIGNAL**

The Germany update does not change that classification.

The new evidence adds variation and strengthens the case against a fixed directional model, but it does not establish a new structural mechanism or a clear break from an established baseline.

> Mechanism confirmed.  
> Direction unresolved.  
> Structural break absent.
