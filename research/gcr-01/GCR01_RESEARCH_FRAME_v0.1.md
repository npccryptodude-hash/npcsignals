# GCR-01 Research Frame v0.1

**Project:** NPCsignals  
**Working title:** The Gen Z Political Break  
**Code:** GCR-01  
**Status:** OPEN / EXPLORATORY  
**Signal status:** UNCLASSIFIED

## Research question

**Is Gen Z becoming a structurally distinct political force across different political systems?**

The test does not assume that Gen Z is left-wing, right-wing, progressive, populist or anti-democratic.

The working hypothesis is narrower:

> Younger cohorts may be less attached to established political channels and more willing to support non-mainstream parties, digital-first movements or direct protest mobilisation.

## Core distinction

GCR-01 separates three outcomes:

1. **Electoral disruption** — younger voters disproportionately support non-mainstream or challenger parties.
2. **Extra-electoral mobilisation** — younger cohorts organise through protests, digital networks or leader-light movements.
3. **Power conversion** — mobilisation produces durable institutional or electoral change.

These must not be treated as equivalent.

## Initial cases

| Case | Observed pattern | Initial role |
|---|---|---|
| Thailand | Strong youth support for Move Forward; age and ideology emerged as major cleavages | Electoral disruption |
| Kenya | Youth-led digital mobilisation forced withdrawal of the 2024 Finance Bill | Extra-electoral mobilisation / policy impact |
| Bangladesh | Gen Z-led uprising helped remove Hasina, but youth-linked NCP won only 6 of 300 seats in 2026 | Mobilisation-power conversion falsification |
| Nepal | 2025 Gen Z protests challenged political legitimacy through digitally mediated mobilisation | Extra-electoral mobilisation |
| Morocco | GenZ 212 protests plus very low youth registration and high youth unemployment | Institutional disengagement / protest |
| Europe | Younger voters often less attached to mainstream parties, but direction varies left/right/independent | Cross-direction electoral disruption |
| Japan | Younger voters can support conservative or establishment-compatible alternatives | Directional falsification / control |

## Global baseline

OECD data show younger adults are generally less engaged in formal political activity such as voting, but more likely than older groups to participate in demonstrations and political activity on social media.

The Inter-Parliamentary Union reports that only 2.8% of parliamentarians worldwide are aged 30 or under, while 37.1% of parliamentary chambers have no members aged 30 or under.

These observations suggest a possible representation gap:

**high youth population / low formal representation / elevated informal mobilisation**

This is a candidate mechanism, not yet a causal conclusion.

## Candidate mechanism

**representation gap / economic pressure / institutional distrust  
→ weak attachment to established parties  
→ digital coordination  
→ challenger preference or protest mobilisation  
→ short-term disruption  
→ uncertain institutional conversion**

## Falsification requirements

GCR-01 must actively search for:

- countries where Gen Z strongly supports incumbents or mainstream parties;
- youth mobilisation that fails to produce policy or electoral consequences;
- historical periods where younger cohorts behaved similarly, implying an age effect rather than a distinct Gen Z effect;
- evidence that economic conditions, education, urbanisation or social media explain the pattern better than generation;
- cases where apparent Gen Z effects disappear after controlling for ideology, class, geography or education.

## Key methodological question

The largest unresolved issue is:

**generation effect vs age effect**

If young people have always been more anti-establishment and become more conventional as they age, GCR-01 is not a structural Gen Z signal.

To qualify as a structural generational break, evidence must show that Gen Z behaves differently from earlier youth cohorts under comparable conditions, or that digital/institutional conditions have changed the political conversion mechanism.
