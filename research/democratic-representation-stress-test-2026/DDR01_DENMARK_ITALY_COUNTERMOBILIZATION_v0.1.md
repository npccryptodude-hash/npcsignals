# DDR-01 Denmark + Italy — Counter-Mobilization Pass v0.1

Status: PROVISIONAL HISTORICAL/MECHANISM ANALYSIS  
Parent module: DDR-01 // Demographic Voting Alignment  
Core MIS framework: UNCHANGED

## Purpose

This pass tests the second DDR-01 mechanism:

> Can immigration or increased ethnic diversity alter elections indirectly by changing how the wider electorate votes?

This is different from asking how immigrant-origin citizens themselves vote.

The Denmark and Italy evidence is especially useful because both countries contain quasi-experimental or causal-identification studies of local immigration exposure and electoral response.

Important:
Most of the strongest causal evidence is historical rather than from the 2024–2026 observation window. It is therefore used as a mechanism test, not as a direct description of current national voting.

---

# Denmark

## 1. Historical causal evidence

Harmon (Scandinavian Journal of Economics) studies Danish municipalities between 1981 and 2001.

Research design:
- local immigration and ethnic-diversity changes;
- instrumental-variable strategy using historical housing-stock variation;
- controls intended to reduce endogenous sorting concerns.

Main finding:
Increases in local ethnic diversity produced a rightward shift in election outcomes:
- lower support for traditional big-government left parties;
- higher support for anti-immigration nationalist parties.

The relationship appeared in both local and national elections.

Interpretation:

This is strong evidence that demographic change can influence the votes of the wider electorate, not merely add new immigrant-origin voters.

## 2. Refugee-allocation evidence

Dustmann, Vasiljeva and Damm's Denmark study uses quasi-random refugee allocation across municipalities.

Main finding:
In municipalities outside the largest urban areas, larger refugee inflows between elections increased support for right-leaning anti-immigration parties.

However:
In the largest urban municipalities, refugee allocation had the opposite or no comparable rightward effect.

The authors link this difference to the sharp urban-rural divide in attitudes toward refugees and possible differences in direct contact.

Interpretation:

> Counter-mobilization is conditional, not automatic.

Exposure can generate a rightward response in some contexts while producing little effect or the opposite response in large urban settings.

## 3. Turnout channel

Danish political research also documents persistent turnout inequality:
- national turnout is generally high;
- non-Western immigrants have substantially lower turnout than the wider electorate.

This creates an important asymmetry:
- immigrant-origin citizens may contribute directly through their own votes;
- immigration exposure may affect a larger native-born electorate indirectly.

Therefore the indirect political effect of immigration can be larger than the direct immigrant-origin vote effect even where immigrants themselves lean toward the left.

## 4. Denmark DDR classification

**DDR6 — COUNTER-MOBILIZATION: SUPPORTED, CONTEXT-DEPENDENT**

The Danish evidence supports a causal rightward response to immigration exposure in several historical settings, but the effect varies by locality and exposure conditions.

Do not write:
> Immigration makes Danish voters move right.

Better:
> Historical Danish causal studies find rightward electoral responses to immigration or refugee exposure in many municipalities, with important urban exceptions.

# Italy

## 1. Earlier causal evidence — broad centre-right response

Barone et al. (Journal of Public Economics, 2016) study Italian municipality-level national-election data.

Using an instrumental-variable design, they find that immigration generated a sizable causal increase in votes for the centre-right coalition.

Mechanisms discussed include:
- cultural diversity;
- labour-market competition;
- competition for public services.

Interpretation:
Italy historically provides strong evidence for an indirect native-voter response to immigration.

## 2. Lega-specific evidence, 2006–2018

A later causal study in the European Journal of Political Economy examines parliamentary election results from 2006 to 2018.

Main finding:
Immigration increased support for Lega and widened Lega's vote gap over its closest competitors.

The effect was stronger in municipalities with lower fiscal autonomy.

The study also finds that Lega's 2018 rebranding from a northern regional party to a national anti-immigration movement increased its ability to convert immigration exposure into votes beyond its old geographic base.

Interpretation:

The political effect of immigration depends not only on demographic exposure but also on party supply:
a party must be capable of organizing and translating the issue into electoral support.

## 3. Refugee-centre proximity

A Journal of Urban Economics study of Italian refugee reception centres found that proximity to reception centres:
- increased turnout;
- increased support for populist parties;
- reduced centre-left support.

The analysis covers the 2013 and 2018 general elections and the 2016 referendum.

This adds a mobilization channel:
immigration exposure can affect not just party preference but whether people participate.

## 4. 2022 evidence complicates the old rule

A 2025 study of the 2022 Italian national election covering nearly all municipalities finds a more mixed relationship.

Main finding:
- across most of Italy, immigrant presence was not significantly associated with higher support for Lega or Fratelli d'Italia;
- in many urban areas, correlations were zero or negative;
- Southern Italy was an important exception, where larger immigrant shares were associated with stronger Fratelli d'Italia support.

Interpretation:

The earlier causal pattern should not be mechanically projected forward.

A mechanism that was strong in 2006–2018 may weaken, saturate or become geographically conditional by 2022.

This is exactly why DDR-01 separates:
- existence of a mechanism;
- persistence of the mechanism;
- present-day magnitude.

## 5. Italy DDR classification

**DDR6 — COUNTER-MOBILIZATION: HISTORICALLY SUPPORTED / CURRENT EFFECT HETEROGENEOUS**

There is credible evidence that immigration contributed causally to rightward/populist shifts in earlier Italian elections.

But newer 2022 evidence does not show a uniform national effect.

Do not write:
> Immigration currently pushes Italy right.

Better:
> Italian causal studies document substantial historical rightward responses to immigration, while 2022 evidence suggests the relationship has become geographically heterogeneous and is no longer uniform nationwide.

# Denmark vs Italy — comparative finding

Both countries support the indirect-channel hypothesis, but both also warn against a universal rule.

Denmark:
- rightward response strongest outside large urban municipalities;
- urban exposure can produce weaker or opposite effects.

Italy:
- strong historical rightward response;
- by 2022, national relationship was mixed and geographically conditional.

This produces an important refinement:

> Immigration exposure can generate counter-mobilization, but the effect depends on prior exposure, geography, contact, party supply and local institutional/economic conditions.

# DDR-01 implications

## DDR-CF15 — Indirect electoral effects can oppose the direct immigrant-origin vote effect

**SUPPORTED**

In a country where immigrant-origin citizens lean left, demographic change can simultaneously increase right-wing support among other voters.

This means the net political effect of demographic change cannot be inferred from immigrant-origin vote choice alone.

## DDR-CF16 — Counter-mobilization is strongest under specific contextual conditions

**SUPPORTED**

The effect is not mechanically proportional to immigrant share.

Evidence points to conditioning by:
- first exposure versus established diversity;
- urban versus rural context;
- centralized/refugee-centre exposure versus dispersed contact;
- party ability to mobilize the issue;
- local fiscal/service conditions.

## DDR-CF17 — Demographic political effects can saturate or reverse

**SUPPORTED AS A MECHANISM**

Denmark and Italy both contain evidence that stronger or longer exposure does not necessarily continue producing ever-larger rightward shifts.

This is consistent with contact effects, adaptation and political saturation.

# Methodological consequence

DDR-01 should never estimate the political effect of demographic change using only:

immigrant-origin electorate size × immigrant-origin vote choice

The correct structure is:

**Total demographic political effect = direct electorate effect + indirect electorate-response effect**

Where:

Direct effect =
eligible immigrant-origin share × turnout × party choice

Indirect effect =
change in the wider electorate's turnout and party choice attributable to demographic/immigration exposure

The second term is much harder to identify and should only be used where credible causal evidence exists.
