# DDR-01 Longitudinal Pass — Sweden and Norway

Status: WORKING NOTE v0.1  
Parent module: DDR-01 // Demographic Voting Alignment  
Core MIS framework: UNCHANGED

## Purpose

This note tests whether immigrant-origin political alignment in Sweden and Norway is stable, strengthening, weakening or fragmenting over time.

The comparison is deliberately descriptive. It does not claim that immigrant-origin voters caused national election outcomes.

---

## Sweden — longitudinal structure

Statistics Sweden (SCB) publishes an official Party Preference Survey series by Swedish/foreign background from 2006 onward.

Important methodological note:
SCB's measure is *partisympati* ("party one feels closest to"), not actual recalled ballot choice. It should therefore be used as a preference trajectory, not as a substitute for certified election results.

Definition used by SCB:
"Foreign background" includes foreign-born people with two foreign-born parents and Sweden-born people with two foreign-born parents.

### Sweden — measured gap in 2026

Foreign background:
- left-green = 65.1
- right/centre-right + SD = 32.9

Swedish background:
- left-green = 44.9
- right/centre-right + SD = 53.1

Difference in left-green preference:
**+20.2 percentage points** among voters with foreign background.

Difference in SD sympathy:
- foreign background: 12.2
- Swedish background: 19.8
- gap: **−7.6 points**

### Sweden — interpretation

The main result is not a simple monotonic trend.

2014 → 2018:
- clear movement away from the very large centre-left advantage
- SD and Moderates both gained substantially among people with foreign background

2018 → 2022/2026:
- centre-left advantage strengthened again
- Social Democrats remained dominant
- Left Party support became particularly strong by 2026

Therefore:

> Foreign-background voters in Sweden have shown persistent left-of-centre alignment, but the strength of that alignment has varied substantially over time.

This is more defensible than saying that the group has moved continuously in one direction.

## Norway — longitudinal structure

Statistics Norway (SSB) provides election-specific studies of eligible voters, turnout and party choice by immigrant background.

The Norwegian series is not methodologically identical to SCB's Swedish party-sympathy series. Norway's evidence is closer to actual election behaviour in the election studies, while Sweden's series measures party sympathy. They should therefore be compared as trajectories, not as directly interchangeable percentages.

### Growth of eligible immigrant-origin electorate

Storting elections:

2005:
- immigrants with voting rights: 111,100
- Norwegian-born with immigrant parents: 11,000

2009:
- immigrants: 154,200
- Norwegian-born with immigrant parents: 18,200

2013:
- immigrants: 185,400
- Norwegian-born with immigrant parents: 28,000

2017:
- immigrants: 233,300
- Norwegian-born with immigrant parents: 41,300

2021:
- immigrants: 288,400
- Norwegian-born with immigrant parents: 56,570

2025:
- immigrants: 391,040
- Norwegian-born with immigrant parents: 75,860

Combined immigrant-origin eligible electorate:
- 2005: ~122,100
- 2013: ~213,400
- 2017: ~274,600
- 2021: ~344,970
- 2025: ~466,900

This is a large structural increase in potential electoral weight.

### 2013

SSB found:
- 55% of voters with immigrant background voted Labour
- 26% voted Conservative
- among voters with background from Africa, Asia and Latin America, more than 60% voted for parties left of centre

Interpretation:
Labour was clearly dominant, but 2013 already showed a visible rightward channel through Conservative support.

### 2017

SSB found:
- close to 60% of voters with background from Africa, Asia etc. voted Labour
- only about 26% of voters without immigrant background voted Labour
- voters from Eastern EU countries leaned more toward the right, especially the Progress Party
- Western-European-background voters looked more like the electorate overall

Interpretation:
Strong left alignment remained, but origin heterogeneity was already substantial.

### 2021

SSB found:
- Labour support among voters from African and Asian backgrounds fell roughly 10 percentage points from 2017
- among Norwegian-born voters with immigrant parents from these regions, Labour support fell roughly 20 points
- much of the gain went to Socialist Left and Red, while some moved to Centre and Liberal
- some former Labour voters abstained

Interpretation:
This was not primarily a simple rightward realignment. It was fragmentation of Labour's old dominance across the broader left plus lower turnout.

### 2025

SSB found:
- Labour remained the largest party among voters with immigrant background
- immigrant-origin voters still voted more for left parties and the Greens than other voters
- Progress Party gained about 9 percentage points among immigrants overall
- Labour fell about 5 points among immigrants overall
- among voters with Asian background, Labour fell about 10 points while the Progress Party gained about 12 points
- immigrant turnout rose from 50% in 2021 to 56% in 2025
- turnout among Norwegian-born with immigrant parents rose from 52% to 60%
- turnout among people without immigrant background was 83%

Interpretation:
2025 provides the clearest evidence so far that the old Norwegian immigrant-origin alignment is becoming less uniform. The change is not complete and Labour remains strongest, but the Progress Party made a meaningful advance, especially among Asian-background voters.

## Sweden vs Norway — first comparative result

### Common pattern

Both countries show:

1. a historically strong left-of-centre advantage among broad immigrant-origin electorates;
2. large internal differences by origin and generation;
3. evidence that the old alignment is not fixed;
4. turnout gaps that materially reduce the electoral weight implied by population share alone.

### Different recent trajectories

**Sweden, pre-election 2026:**
The broad foreign-background electorate showed a strong renewed left-green preference gap relative to voters with Swedish background.

**Norway, 2025 election:**
The left remained stronger among immigrant-origin voters, but the Progress Party recorded a significant advance and Labour lost ground in several origin groups.

This means the two neighbouring countries do not show one common directional trend.

> Sweden in 2026 looks more like persistence/re-strengthening of a left-aligned demographic coalition.  
> Norway in 2025 looks more like fragmentation of an older left-aligned coalition.

That divergence is analytically useful.

## DDR-01 implications

### DDR-CF06 — Historical left alignment can persist without being stable

**SUPPORTED by Sweden and Norway**

A demographic bloc may remain left-leaning over decades while still undergoing large internal shifts between parties and across origin groups.

### DDR-CF07 — Growth in eligible electorate does not equal proportional political impact

**SUPPORTED**

Norway's immigrant-origin eligible electorate grew from roughly 122,000 in 2005 to roughly 467,000 in 2025, but turnout remained substantially below the rest of the electorate.

Therefore demographic weight must always be decomposed into:

eligible share × turnout × party preference

—not population share alone.

### DDR-CF08 — Neighbouring systems can diverge despite similar migration-era pressures

**SUPPORTED AS DESCRIPTIVE FINDING**

Sweden and Norway do not currently show the same trajectory:
- Sweden: stronger left-green preference gap by 2026
- Norway: greater cross-bloc fragmentation by 2025

This weakens any simple regional rule such as "immigrant-origin electorates in Scandinavia are moving left" or "moving right."

## Next step

The next pass should estimate outcome relevance rather than just preference alignment.

For Sweden 2026:
1. estimate eligible electorate with foreign background;
2. estimate turnout;
3. apply observed party/bloc preference with uncertainty;
4. estimate net centre-left contribution relative to Swedish-background voters;
5. compare with the final national bloc margin.

For Norway 2025:
1. estimate actual votes cast by immigrant-origin electorate;
2. estimate net left/right contribution;
3. compare 2021 and 2025;
4. decompose how much of the shift came from turnout and how much from party-choice change.

Only after that should DDR7 — Outcome Relevance — be assigned.
