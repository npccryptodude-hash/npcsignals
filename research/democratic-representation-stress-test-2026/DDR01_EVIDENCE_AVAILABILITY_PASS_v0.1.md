## Purpose

This pass does not search for evidence that confirms the DDR-01 mechanism.

It asks a narrower question:

> Is there enough comparable evidence in France, Poland, Costa Rica, Chile and Japan to include each country in the main DDR-01 comparative analysis?

The inclusion threshold remains:
- defensible population definition;
- national-election voting eligibility documented;
- turnout and/or party-choice evidence;
- source quality high enough to separate description from inference;
- no forced substitution of weak proxies for missing vote-choice data.

# France

## What is available

INSEE provides strong demographic data on:
- immigrants;
- foreigners;
- naturalization;
- descendants of immigrants;
- civic participation.

In 2024:
- 6.0 million foreign nationals lived in France;
- 33.5% of immigrants had acquired French nationality;
- 8.0 million descendants of immigrants lived in France outside Mayotte.

INSEE's civic-participation work also shows turnout differences by immigrant/descendant status and by origin.

For the 2017 presidential election, among French nationals aged 18–59 who reported being registered:
- reported turnout was 85% among people neither immigrant nor descendant of immigrants;
- 82% among immigrants;
- 79% among descendants of immigrants.

Turnout varied substantially by origin.

CEVIPOF also has large election-panel infrastructure, including the 2022 French Electoral Survey, designed to test individual and contextual determinants of vote choice.

## What is missing for DDR-01

The current source set does not yet provide a clean, nationally representative, directly comparable 2022/2024 party-choice table or validated-voter model by immigrant-origin status equivalent to:
- Norway SSB;
- Sweden SCB;
- US Pew;
- Germany KAS.

France's official statistical system can measure migration origin, but public election-vote data by ethnicity/origin are more constrained and fragmented across surveys.

## France decision

**DO NOT PROMOTE TO MAIN DDR3 COMPARISON YET**

Evidence status:
- DDR1: strong demographic base
- DDR2: usable turnout evidence
- DDR3: OPEN / private survey reconstruction required
- DDR4: likely measurable
- DDR5: open
- DDR6: separate research question
- DDR7: not assignable

Tier:
**B/C FOR PARTICIPATION / D FOR CURRENT PARTY-CHOICE COMPARABILITY**

Working status:
**PARTIAL ENTRY — TURNOUT TRACK ONLY**

# Poland

## What is available

OECD comparative integration data can measure turnout among immigrants who hold host-country nationality.

Poland is unusual in those older comparative data because immigrant-naturalized turnout was not lower than native-born turnout.

However, that source:
- pools older periods;
- measures participation rather than party choice;
- predates much of Poland's recent immigration increase.

There is strong research on Polish emigrants voting in Polish elections and on how emigration changes domestic outcomes.

That literature is not directly relevant to DDR-01's host-country immigrant-origin electorate question.

## What is missing

No sufficiently strong, recent national dataset was located that estimates:
- party choice among naturalized/immigrant-origin voters resident in Poland;
- origin-specific party alignment;
- trend over repeated Polish elections.

The currently identifiable literature is dominated by:
- Polish diaspora voting abroad;
- immigration as a campaign issue;
- migration policy attitudes;
rather than voting behaviour of immigrant-origin Polish citizens.

## Poland decision

**REMAIN OUTSIDE MAIN DDR-01 COMPARISON**

Tier:
**D — INSUFFICIENT CURRENT HOST-COUNTRY PARTY-CHOICE DATA**

Working status:
**DO NOT INFER**

Important:
Polish diaspora voting must not be substituted for immigrant-origin voting inside Poland.

# Costa Rica

## What is available

Costa Rica's Supreme Electoral Tribunal (TSE) provides unusually strong administrative election infrastructure.

The 2026 electoral register can be explored by:
- province;
- canton;
- district;
- sex;
- age;
- new voters;
- naturalized citizens by place of birth;
- overseas voters.

Costa Rican electoral law also establishes that naturalized Costa Rican citizens may vote after the statutory post-naturalization waiting period.

This means DDR1 can potentially be measured very well using administrative records.

## What is missing

No sufficiently strong source was located that links:
- naturalized status or country of birth
to
- actual party/candidate vote choice

at the individual level.

The TSE register can identify naturalized-electorate composition, but the secret ballot prevents administrative linkage to vote choice.

A survey source would therefore be required for DDR3.

## Costa Rica decision

**PROMOTE ELECTORATE-SIZE TRACK, NOT PARTY-ALIGNMENT TRACK**

Evidence status:
- DDR1: potentially strong administrative data
- DDR2: open
- DDR3: insufficient
- DDR4: origin distribution available administratively
- DDR5: potentially reconstructable over registries
- DDR6: open
- DDR7: not assignable

Tier:
**A FOR ELECTORATE COMPOSITION / D FOR PARTY CHOICE**

Working status:
**PARTIAL ENTRY — ELECTORATE STRUCTURE ONLY**

# Chile

## What is available

Chile is materially stronger than the first DDR-01 audit suggested.

Unlike most comparison countries, Chile allows qualifying foreign nationals to vote without naturalization.

Servel states that foreign residents may vote after more than five years of qualifying residence, subject to constitutional/legal conditions.

For the provisional 2025 electoral register:
- total eligible voters: 15,791,056
- foreign-national eligible voters: 886,190

Largest foreign-national electorates:
- Venezuela: 237,889
- Peru: 193,883
- Colombia: 103,239

This means foreign nationals represented roughly 5.6% of the provisional electorate.

Peer-reviewed research is unusually strong for turnout.

Herrera & Morales use:
- a 2017 administrative census with 267,116 migrant electors plus a survey of 4,771 migrants;
- a later administrative census covering more than 70 million voter-election observations and about 1.2 million migrant observations from 2012–2020.

The studies identify systematic turnout differences by:
- sex;
- age;
- registration history;
- country-of-origin institutions;
- family/social integration.

A 2026 administrative-census study of party membership and voting reports:
- nationals were much more likely than foreign-born residents to belong to political parties;
- nationals voted substantially more often than foreign-born residents;
- among foreign-born party members, party membership leaned more left;
- turnout among foreign-born party members was highest among those affiliated with right-wing parties.

## Important limitation

Party membership is not the same as ballot choice.

The evidence is therefore much stronger for:
- DDR1 electorate share;
- DDR2 turnout;
- DDR4 heterogeneity;
than for DDR3 national party vote choice.

## Chile decision

**PROMOTE FROM D TO B/C PARTIAL ENTRY**

Evidence status:
- DDR1: A — administrative electorate data
- DDR2: A — administrative turnout research
- DDR3: C / proxy only through party membership, not ballot choice
- DDR4: strong evidence
- DDR5: turnout trajectory measurable
- DDR6: open
- DDR7: not assignable without vote-choice data

Working status:
**STRONG PARTICIPATION CASE / PARTY-ALIGNMENT STILL UNRESOLVED**

This is the largest upgrade produced by this availability pass.

# Japan

## What is available

Japanese citizens, including naturalized citizens, have national voting rights.

Foreign permanent residents without Japanese citizenship do not have national voting rights.

Japan has a sizeable foreign-resident population, but naturalization is a much smaller and analytically distinct category.

Current political reporting also shows that immigration/foreign-resident issues became more electorally salient in 2025, including the rise of Sanseito on a "Japanese First" platform.

This may be relevant to DDR6 as a possible indirect electorate-response mechanism.

## What is missing

No robust national source was located that provides:
- naturalized-citizen party preference;
- naturalized-citizen turnout compared with native-born citizens;
- repeated national-election vote-choice series by nativity/origin.

Available material is mostly:
- legal/institutional;
- anecdotal;
- foreign-resident politics;
- general immigration attitudes;
- party/campaign response to immigration.

That is insufficient for DDR3.

The rise of anti-foreigner rhetoric also does not by itself establish that immigration exposure caused vote changes.

## Japan decision

**REMAIN OUTSIDE MAIN DIRECT DDR-01 COMPARISON**

Tier:
**D FOR DIRECT VOTE-CHOICE ANALYSIS**

DDR6:
**WATCH ONLY — CURRENT POLITICAL SALIENCE, CAUSAL EFFECT NOT IDENTIFIED**

Working status:
**INSUFFICIENT DIRECT DATA / INDIRECT CHANNEL WATCH**

# Implications for DDR-01

## 1. Chile changes status

The original first-pass classification of Chile as simply "insufficient data" was too pessimistic.

Chile has:
- a large measurable foreign-national electorate;
- unusual voting eligibility rules;
- administrative turnout evidence;
- peer-reviewed migrant political-participation research.

It should now enter the comparative module as a **participation/eligibility case**, while DDR3 remains unresolved.

## 2. Missing party-choice data is a real recurring boundary

France, Costa Rica, Chile and Japan all illustrate a key distinction:

A country may have excellent data on:
- who is eligible;
- who votes;
- origin;
- participation;

while still lacking defensible evidence about:
- which party those voters choose.

DDR-01 should not substitute turnout or party membership for ballot choice.

## 3. Poland remains a clean null

The search returned meaningful work on:
- Polish emigrants;
- migration politics;
- immigration attitudes;

but not the host-country immigrant-origin party-choice evidence required by the frozen DDR-01 design.

This should remain a documented data null.
