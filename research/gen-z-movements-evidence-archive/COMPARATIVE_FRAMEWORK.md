# comparative framework

## research question

what happens after a youth movement becomes large enough to affect national politics?

## four forms of power

### 1 / mobilization power
can the movement bring large numbers of people into coordinated action?

### 2 / organizational power
can it maintain leadership, logistics, decision rules, funding and continuity after the initial trigger?

### 3 / electoral power
can it convert public support into votes, candidates and representation?

### 4 / institutional power
can it govern, reform institutions and sustain change once inside the state?

## current comparison

| case | mobilization | organization | electoral | institutional outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| thailand | high | fragmented / repressed | limited | long-tail repression; durable reform not established |
| bangladesh | very high | sufficient for regime challenge | weak for youth-led successor party | transition occurred, but established parties dominated election |
| serbia | very high | durable | majority | live institutional test |
| france | high | evolving | not applicable yet | unresolved / live case |

## working conclusion

**mobilization power ≠ organizational power ≠ electoral power ≠ institutional power**

success at one stage does not prove success at the next.

## comparison cautions

- do not treat all youth movements as ideologically equivalent;
- do not assume similar tactics mean shared coordination;
- distinguish government claims from independently verified evidence;
- compare mechanisms, not just crowd size;
- identify whether a movement is school-led, university-led, cross-generational or party-led at each stage.
