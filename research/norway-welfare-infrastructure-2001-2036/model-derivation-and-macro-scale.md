# Model Derivation and Macro Scale

Freeze date: **2026-09-30**

This note explains why the NOK 250 billion ceiling exists and how its scale compares with current fiscal aggregates.

## 1. A derived lower and upper frame

The research should not treat NOK 250 billion as a free-standing anchor.

A more transparent construction is:

### A. Gross restoration core

Working ranges already used in the audit:

- Schools: **NOK 75–90 billion** — derived working range
- Hospitals: **NOK 67 billion** — RIF gross technical estimate
- Existing elderly-care / institutional buildings: **NOK 25–35 billion** — derived proxy

This produces a **gross restoration core of roughly NOK 167–192 billion**.

This is not incremental funding. Existing investment must still be deducted.

### B. Future elderly-care capacity

Statistics Norway has projected a need for about **18,000 additional round-the-clock care places by 2035**.

Husbanken’s 2026 maximum approved project cost is approximately:

- **NOK 4.272 million per unit** outside designated high-cost areas
- **NOK 4.886 million per unit** in high-cost areas

A pure scale calculation gives:

- 18,000 × 4.272m = **NOK 76.9 billion**
- 18,000 × 4.886m = **NOK 87.9 billion**

This is **not** a forecast of actual state expenditure.

The 18,000 places include different service forms, actual project costs vary, and grants cover only part of approved costs.

### C. Equipment / technical reset

Working reserve:

**NOK 5–10 billion**

### D. Full gross-scope scale

Gross restoration core: **NOK 167–192bn**  
Future care-capacity scale: **NOK 76.9–87.9bn**  
Equipment / technical reserve: **NOK 5–10bn**

Combined gross scale:

> **approximately NOK 249–290 billion**

This gives a transparent reason for retaining **NOK 250 billion as a maximum programme ceiling**: it sits near the lower end of a full-scope gross scenario, while existing funded programmes should reduce the actual incremental requirement.

The correct sequence remains:

> **gross need − already-funded relevant investment = incremental need**

## 2. Macro scale against the 2026 fiscal framework

The Revised National Budget 2026 estimates:

- structural non-oil fiscal deficit: **NOK 579 billion**
- equivalent to **12.6% of trend GDP for mainland Norway**
- equivalent to **2.7% of GPFG value**

From those published figures, trend mainland GDP is approximately:

> 579 / 0.126 = **NOK 4.60 trillion**

### If NOK 250bn were spread evenly over ten years

Average programme activity:

> **NOK 25 billion per year**

Relative to the 2026 fiscal aggregates:

- about **0.54% of trend mainland GDP**
- about **4.3% of the 2026 structural non-oil deficit**

If the entire NOK 25bn were treated mechanically as additional fund-financed state expenditure, the arithmetic fund-use ratio would rise from about **2.70% to 2.82%**.

That is **not** a forecast of the actual fiscal effect.

The programme is not expected to be:
- evenly phased,
- entirely state-financed,
- entirely incremental,
- entirely financed by additional GPFG transfers.

### Under the modelled 55% direct-state-capital share

Average direct state capital would be:

> **NOK 13.75 billion per year**

Against the same 2026 denominators, that is approximately:

- **0.30% of trend mainland GDP**
- **2.37% of the 2026 structural non-oil deficit**

A purely mechanical fund-use comparison would move 2.70% to roughly **2.76%**.

Again, this is a scale illustration, not a budget forecast.

## 3. What this calculation can and cannot show

It **can** show that the tested programme is material but not of a scale that is obviously incompatible with current national fiscal aggregates.

It **cannot** establish that the programme is automatically affordable or macroeconomically neutral.

The actual impact depends on:

- baseline subtraction
- annual phasing
- offsets elsewhere in the budget
- construction capacity
- labour availability
- inflation
- interest rates
- financing mix
- the composition of spending

The fiscal-rule percentage should therefore remain context, not the programme’s funding formula.
