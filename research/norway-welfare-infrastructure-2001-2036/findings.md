# Findings

## Finding 1 — Aggregate national financial scarcity is not a sufficient explanation

**Status: SUPPORTED**

Norway’s public financial capacity expanded dramatically over the 2001–2025 period while substantial public investment continued.

This does not mean every desirable project could have been financed at once. It means **aggregate national financial scarcity** fits poorly as the primary explanation. Local and sector-specific scarcity can remain real because financial capacity and asset responsibility are distributed differently across the system.

## Finding 2 — Large investment and technical backlog can coexist

**Status: CONFIRMED / SUPPORTED**

Municipalities continue to invest heavily in buildings, while documented technical upgrade needs remain large.

The relevant distinction is between:

- new construction
- capacity expansion
- replacement
- rehabilitation
- routine lifecycle maintenance

## Finding 3 — Deferred maintenance is an important contributing mechanism

**Status: SUPPORTED AS A MATERIAL CONTRIBUTOR**

The evidence is consistent with the following mechanism:

> **maintenance is deferred → technical debt accumulates → rehabilitation becomes more expensive later**

This does not explain the entire backlog.

## Finding 4 — Restoration and future capacity must be separated

**Status: STRONGLY SUPPORTED**

This is especially important in elderly care, where population ageing creates additional capacity demand even if today’s assets are fully restored.

## Finding 5 — School infrastructure and PISA are separate evidence questions

**Status: CONFIRMED / NOT ESTABLISHED**

Norway’s educational outcomes have deteriorated substantially.

The research does not establish that school-building condition caused the national PISA decline.

## Finding 6 — NOK 250 billion is a maximum programme envelope

**Status: MODELLED**

The figure is used to stress-test whether a ten-year programme could fit within Norway’s existing fiscal architecture.

A derived gross restoration core is approximately **NOK 167–192 billion**. Updated SSB projections now estimate about **22,800 additional round-the-clock care places from 2024 to 2035**. Using 2026 Husbanken maximum approved project-cost levels as a scale test and adding a small equipment reserve produces a full gross-scope scale of approximately **NOK 269–313 billion**.

This update changes the interpretation of NOK 250 billion: it is retained as a **modelled maximum incremental programme envelope**, not as the lower edge of full gross scope. Whether it is sufficient depends on baseline subtraction, prioritisation and future service mix.

See [model-derivation-and-macro-scale.md](model-derivation-and-macro-scale.md).

## Finding 6A — Saving has an opportunity cost, but so can delay

**Status: SUPPORTED AS A DECISION FRAMEWORK**

The opportunity cost of spending is real: capital used today gives up future financial returns.

For necessary infrastructure, however, delay can also carry a compounding cost through higher future rehabilitation expense, secondary damage, shorter useful life, higher operating costs and service disruption.

The correct comparison is therefore not “save versus spend”, but:

> **financial compounding versus technical compounding**

Major projects should include an explicit **Cost of Delay** assessment alongside foregone financial return.

## Finding 7 — Preservation is as important as restoration

**Status: STRONG PROGRAMME REQUIREMENT**

> **Do not finance restoration without financing preservation.**

A restoration programme that does not establish funded lifecycle maintenance risks recreating the same backlog.
